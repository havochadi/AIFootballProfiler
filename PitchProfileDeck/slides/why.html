Player data without the cost of collecting it

Football and data

Why football: it reaches billions. About 5 billion people engaged with the 2022 World Cup.

How statistics help: clubs use them to find players, plan matches and manage workload.

In practice: Brentford pairs data with scouting to find undervalued players.

How it improves

Today: trained operators tag events by hand. Accurate, but it takes people.
Now: found from the video.

Today: about 30 cameras per stadium, or GPS vests for one team.
Now: one video, both teams.

Today: youth and women's matches are mostly not covered.
Now: any match on video.

How it can be used

Scouting: compare players from match video.

Match review: team shape, passing and pressing.

Clubs without a data feed: academies, women's and lower leagues.

Not new: broadcast-video tracking is already sold (for example by SkillCorner), and one 2026 study found it close to stadium cameras for most events. This is an open research prototype, and for now a complement.

About 60 seconds. WHY FOOTBALL: FIFA reports around 5 billion people engaged with the 2022 World Cup, close to 1.5 billion reaching the final. "Engaged" is FIFA's broad measure of following tournament content, not unique viewers. HOW STATISTICS HELP. Recruitment: a survey of 19 professionals found clubs use video and data to evaluate players, find overlooked talent in external datasets and profile strengths and weaknesses (Frost, Groom and Nicholls, 2025, International Journal of Sports Science and Coaching). Tactics: Rein and Memmert (2016, SpringerPlus) review how tracking and event data support tactical analysis, where the old gold standard was expert observation, which is subjective and time-consuming. Workload: GPS data are used to monitor training load for each player and the team, linked to injury risk (Ravé et al., 2020, Frontiers in Physiology; an opinion paper, and the workload-injury link is debated). Example: Brentford uses statistical models to look for players whose potential exceeds their price, and its owner says there is still old-fashioned scouting alongside (ESPN, Sky Sports). HOW EVENTS ARE RECORDED TODAY: match events are collected with proprietary tagging software by human analysts, and the data is owned by the companies that collect it (Pappalardo et al., 2019, Scientific Data). Stats Perform, which owns Opta, describes highly trained analysts applying 60-plus event definitions. This is accurate: two groups of experienced operators agreed with kappa 0.92 and 0.94 on team events, though on one match (Liu et al., 2013). So the cost is people, not accuracy. TRACKING: Second Spectrum is the Premier League's official optical provider since 2019, and Genius Sports has described roughly 28 to 30 cameras at every Premier League ground. Wearable GPS vests were approved for matches in 2015 but cover your own team only. COVERAGE: the analytics firm Driblab says coverage of youth and women's competitions is non-existent in most countries, and SkillCorner says academy and women's matches often have only a single camera; both are vendor statements and I found no independent figure. NOT NEW: SkillCorner already sells tracking from broadcast video. Independent evidence: Mills et al. (2026, Sports Engineering) compared broadcast-derived tracking with the stadium TRACAB system and FIFA's manually tagged events for one 2022 World Cup match, and in the best case it matched or came within 0.05 F1 of the stadium system for most events, limited by player visibility, ball tracking errors and subjective event definitions. One match only. So a complement for now. Full links: PitchProfileDeck/SOURCES.md.
